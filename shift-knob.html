Shift Knob

Current Project, Work in Progress

This project was to design a shift-knob for my Mazda MX-5. I started with three design requirements:

  1. It must have a customizable component to it so the user can customize it to their preference
  2. Said customizable piece must be a simple and durable design so that it can be reproduced in a variety of different materials (Plastic, wood, metal, etc.)
  3. Must be of a reasonable cost to produce (<$100 would put it inline with many aftermarket options, but <$120 is a more realistic goal as I am not mass-producing)

1st Prototype:

The first prototype, and all the others, were designed in Solidworks and printed on a Bambulabs X1 mini in PLA. This one is composed of two pieces that bolt to each other with 4 M2 screws. The top piece would be the simple part that the user could replace if they wish to customize the knob. As far as the initial design, I wanted to make the top (spherical piece) out of a plastic for the final version and the bottom out of some metal. The reason I opted for a plastic for the top over some type of metal was that metals conduct heat much better than plastics (generally speaking), and when you park your car outside for a long time, a metal shift knob can become extremely hot to the touch which is uncomfortable or even dangerous. Only the bottom portion is threaded through the main (large) hole and the top portion used 4 triserts (threaded inserts) that screw into the plastic, for the smaller screws to thread into. I did this since M2's are quite small and I could foresee them being difficult for me to tap. The main thread on the prototype was also tapped, not 3D-printed.

There was one glaring issue with this design: since I threaded the bottom portion, not the top, there was a large gap between the shift-boot (leather part on car) and the shift-knob.

2nd Prototype:

For the 2nd prototype, I made the adjustment of making the main hole on the bottom piece a clearance hole and then threading the top piece instead, allowing the shift-knob to sit lower on the shifter to get rid of this gap. I also added a chamfer at the bottom to fit over the chamfer on the shifter, keeping things flush.

As you can see above, it sat flush which was great. However, after this iteration I noticed a few improvements that should be made. Aesthetically, I thought it would be better if the bottom of the shift knob was as wide as the top of the shift-boot for a more seamless look. More importantly, I worried that the steel shifter screwing directly into the plastic top piece would be too weak and reduce the longevity of the shift-knob (compared to before where it screwed into the metal bottom piece).

3rd Prototype:

For the 3rd prototype, I made some significant changes to both pieces to solve these issues. I added an extension to the metal bottom piece that would allow it to hold the main threads instead of the top part that I intended to make out of plastic. This added complexity to the bottom piece but actually made the top piece simpler (since the threads were moved to the bottom). This was ideal because the top was the part that I intended to make swappable by the user, so I wanted it to be simple so that new designs would be easier to make and manufacture.

However, after this prototype, I noticed one small improvement I could make before sending the final version to get manufactured. This added shaft would allow me to have some splines on it which would relieve some of the torque on the small M2 screws, increasing the strength of the connection. This would also have the added benefit of making more of the overall volume of the shift-knob made out of metal, increasing the weight of the product. Generally, more weight is better for shifting as it makes the shifter go into gear easier and dampens some of the vibrations coming through the shifter forks to the driver's hand.

Assembled Product:

Upon trying to install the shift-knob onto the car, I ran into an issue. The threads were catching after about 1 turn. I believe this is a manufacturing defect. Since I could thread the knob through all of the threads from the other end of the hole, I knew it was just a small defect affecting only a couple of threads. First, I tried to thread an M10 x 1.25 tap through the threads to see if I could fix the affected threads, but I was not able to. However I broke my tap on the affected threads by trying to do this so I needed to find a different method (I was able to use a tap extractor to remove the broken piece from the hole).

To resolve the issue I bored out the first 2-3 turns worth of threads. This would have no noticeable effect on the performance of the shift-knob as there was still plenty of threads left to make contact. I used a 13/32" cobalt drill bit which is the closest commonly available drill bit size to the original hole. It is however slightly larger than the original hole (only 0.1mm larger) but not enough to affect the performance. Below is a video of me machining the piece to remove the defective threads.

Finally, I decided to polish the visible surfaces of the metal piece on a buffing wheel using an aggressive compound and a fine compound. This was my first time polishing metal so I wanted to go for a natural but not super high polish finish as I did not want to try and remove too much material. And then the product was finished!
